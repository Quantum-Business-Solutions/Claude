# Topic Arcs

A topic arc is the sequence of subjects across the emails. A bad arc means each email is fine in isolation but the program doesn't add up to anything. A good arc creates momentum — the reader anticipates the next one.

## What makes a topic strong

Strong email topics share these traits:

1. **Names a specific pain point**, not a category. "Your CRM is lying to you about pipeline" beats "How to improve your CRM."
2. **Has an opinionated POV**, not neutral coverage. "Most lead scoring is broken (here's the fix)" beats "Best practices for lead scoring."
3. **Speaks to a job role**, not "businesses." Emails written for "RevOps leaders" or "office equipment dealers" land harder than emails written for "B2B companies."
4. **Sounds like the company would actually say it**. If the headline could appear on any HubSpot partner blog, it's too generic.

## Weak topics to avoid

- "10 tips for [generic topic]"
- "The ultimate guide to [thing]"
- "What is [concept]"
- "How [tool] can help you [outcome]"
- "Top trends in [industry] for [year]"
- Anything that starts with "Welcome to..." or "Introducing..."

These trigger inbox-skim behavior and signal the email is generic content marketing.

## Proven arc patterns

Pick the pattern that fits the campaign goal.

### Pattern 1: Foundation → Tactical → Strategic

Used for top-of-funnel nurture to a broad B2B audience.

```
Email 1: A foundational POV ("most companies get [X] wrong")
Emails 2–4: Specific tactical fixes
Emails 5–7: Harder/bigger problems
Email 8+: Forward-looking / planning / decision-driving
```

Example (from the QBS HubSpot Services nurture):
1. The #1 HubSpot mistake costing you deals
2. Your CRM is lying to you about pipeline
3. The 5-motion Revenue Efficiency Model
4. 5 HubSpot features 90% of customers never turn on
5. Sequences vs Workflows — stop using the wrong one
6. ... etc.

### Pattern 2: Problem → Agitate → Solution

Used for product launches or new service announcements (typically 5–8 emails).

```
Email 1: Name the problem clearly
Email 2: Show the cost of ignoring it
Email 3: Why most attempted solutions fail
Email 4: A better approach (introduce the offering)
Email 5: Specific features / case study
Email 6: Decision call (book demo)
Email 7: Last call / urgency
```

### Pattern 3: Industry POV series

Used for thought leadership — positioning the sender as the authority in a specific niche.

```
Each email is a contrarian or deeply opinionated POV on a different aspect of the industry.
No tight narrative arc — each email stands alone.
Best when the audience has 1–2 minutes of attention and trust hasn't been built yet.
```

### Pattern 4: Mini-course / educational sequence

Used when there's a complex topic worth teaching across multiple emails.

```
Email 1: Why this matters (hook)
Emails 2-4: Each covers one concept building on the prior
Email 5: How it all fits together
Email 6: How to implement / how the company helps
```

This works better when the audience opted in specifically for the content (e.g., from a lead magnet).

## How many emails per arc

| Length | When to use |
|---|---|
| 3–5 emails | Quick campaign, single product launch, time-bound offer |
| 6–10 emails | Standard nurture, mini-course, evergreen sequence |
| 11–20 emails | Long-form thought leadership program (multi-month) |
| 20+ emails | Only when the audience is highly engaged and the topic is broad |

Default to **5 emails** if the user hasn't specified. It's enough to test the pattern without committing to a 6-month program if the first emails underperform.

## Topic generation process

1. **Start with the campaign goal**. Every topic must plausibly motivate that goal. If the goal is "book HubSpot consultations," don't include a topic that's just educational with no hook back to consulting.

2. **List 3x the topics you need** (15 topics for a 5-email program). It's easier to cut than to invent.

3. **Score each topic** against three criteria:
   - **Specificity** (1–5): how narrow and concrete is the pain point?
   - **POV strength** (1–5): does it have a real opinion, or just describe?
   - **Goal-fit** (1–5): does it plausibly drive the desired action?

4. **Sort by total score** and pick the top N.

5. **Sequence them** using one of the patterns above.

6. **Show the user before writing any email body**. Get confirmation on the arc before producing copy. This is the single most important checkpoint — bad topic = bad email no matter how well written.

## Special case: AI/Claude topics for QBS

When QBS is the sender and the topic involves AI:

- **Position expertise as essential, not replaceable**. Frame AI as a force multiplier for experts, not a substitute for them.
- **Avoid claims that "AI replaced" QBS staff or services**. This trains prospects to think they don't need QBS.
- **Use risk/safety framing for client-facing AI emails**. "What goes wrong when AI ships unsupervised" beats "What AI can do."
- **Always end on Quantum being the safety net** that catches what AI alone misses.

This positioning protects the relationship and keeps the AI conversation a reason to engage Quantum, not skip them.
