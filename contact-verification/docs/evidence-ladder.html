QBS · Contact Data Operations

The Evidence Ladder

A repeatable process for validating any calling list — built from what a real list taught us, including the contact it nearly deleted.

Portal 20682069 Pilot: list 3625 · CAS Deal Follow up 183 contacts 17 Aug 2026

The thesis

Rank your evidence before you spend money on it. Every contact question — is this person still here, does this number work, is this email real — has answers at four very different levels of trust. The cheapest and most reliable answers are already inside your own systems. The expensive ones are the least trustworthy.

The pilot run got this backwards, and it nearly cost a live deal. Fixing the order is the process.

Four tiers of proof

Work top down. Stop as soon as the question is answered. Never let a lower tier overrule a higher one.

1 Your own interactionsOutlook mail and calendar, meeting recaps, ConnectAndSell session reports, logged calls. Email signatures carry corrected phones and addresses. First-party
2 Recorded outcomes & sibling recordsHard bounces, dial dispositions, reply history — facts about what happened when you tried. Plus the email convention of every colleague at the same domain, which validates or refutes any address for free. Observed
3 The person's own page — once identity is provenLinkedIn and Sales Navigator answer "still in this seat." But a stored LinkedIn URL is usually a guess made by an enrichment tool. Corroborate the profile against title, employer and city before believing a word of it. 2 of the first 13 checked were a different human entirely. Observed
4 Purchased data — the fallback, not the checkReach for it only once tier 3 is exhausted: the profile is locked, or there is no profile at all. Never use it to verify data it supplied in the first place — that is circular, and the same vendor writing your jobtitle is the one oscillating it. When you do use it, read contactAccuracyScore, validDate and lastUpdatedDate, and treat a match with no current company as "departed, destination unknown". Vendor
5 Guesses dressed as findingsField is blank so the fact is absent. Area code differs so the number is wrong. A record looks thin so it's junk. Never a verdict. Inference

The case that rewrote the process

The pilot flagged this contact as junk on three counts and queued him for deletion. Tier-1 evidence — sitting in the founder's own inbox — reversed every one.

William "Bill" Burfeind Chief Revenue Officer · Proven IT · Tinley Park, IL · contact 119401
Email bill.burfeind@provenit.com wburfeind@provenit.com The CRM guessed a first.last pattern. That guess is the entire reason the address hard-bounced — which the pipeline then read as evidence the person was gone. His correct address appears in every reply he has ever sent.
Phone +1 312-961-7578 · mobile, DNC-flagged 708-407-2422 · direct line His direct dial has been in his email signature since January. The CRM holds only the flagged mobile, duplicated across both phone fields, so he looked unreachable.
Employment inferred departed CRO since April 2021 · no end date Five years in seat. First-degree connection with 25 shared connections.
Relationship no company association two meetings · asked to continue Met 28 Jan 2026 — recap records him discussing Proven IT's HubSpot usage, CRM integration problems, and bringing in their CTO. Met again 4 Feb. He rescheduled once with "I definitely want to revisit your…" and appears in the 8 Jul ConnectAndSell session report.

What this proves. Three vendor and inference signals all pointed one direction and all three were wrong. One tier-1 source — an email signature — held both the corrected email and the missing phone number.

He is not a record to clean. He is an engaged CRO at a real dealer who has asked twice to keep talking, and the right next move is neither a dial nor an email to the bounced address: it is a LinkedIn message, where the connection already exists.

The second case: contamination flows downhill

Burfeind showed that vendor data can be wrong. This one shows something worse — that a guessed field can overwrite a correct one, and then be mistaken for evidence.

Michael Macri VP of Sales · Fraser Advanced Information Systems · Bala Cynwyd, PA · contact 89730991232
Company i-Path Media Fraser Advanced Information Systems An enrichment integration matched him to /in/michael-macri — a different Michael Macri, VP at a pharma-marketing firm in Doylestown, PA. Same name, same state. That wrong profile's employer then overwrote the company field.
LinkedIn /in/michael-macri /in/michael-macri-06580025 The real profile reads "Vice President of Sales at Fraser Advanced Information Systems," located Bala-Cynwyd PA — matching this record's own city field, with 29 shared connections.
Email flagged as wrong company mmacri@fraser-ais.com — correct It matches Fraser's flast convention, used by 18+ colleagues. The address was right all along; the bounce is a mailbox problem, not a wrong address and not a departure.
Job title read as truncated "Vice President, Sales — Fraser Advanced Information Systems" The field named the right employer the entire time. It was clipped to 25 characters in the triage output, so nobody read it.

Asserted fields versus derived fields. Some fields are asserted — a human typed them, or they came from a real exchange: the email someone replied from, the title they gave, the city on their record. Others are derived — an integration inferred them. Here a derived field silently overwrote an asserted one, and the corruption then looked like evidence.

This is the concrete form of a problem already recorded against this portal: enrichment automation overwriting verified data. Any hygiene process that does not know which of its fields are asserted and which are derived will keep laundering guesses into facts.

The decision, contact by contact

Every contact walks the same five questions. Each one that answers early costs nothing further — that is the whole economy of the thing.

The pipeline

Six stages. Stages 1 and 2 are free and answer most questions. Everything paid runs only on what survives them.

Free · no external calls

Harvest first-party evidence

Search Outlook mail and calendar for every name and domain on the list. Parse email signatures for direct dials, titles, and addresses. Pull logged meetings and the ConnectAndSell session reports that arrive from system.reports@connectandsell.com.

Anyone you have met or exchanged mail with is verified by definition — no vendor needed. This stage was skipped in the pilot and is the single largest change.

Free · two batch reads

Read what the CRM already knows

Presence of email, phone, and LinkedIn per channel. Bounce history. Dial dispositions from associated call records — the pilot list has 22,971 logged calls across 181 of its 183 contacts, an untouched record of which numbers actually connect.

Read every field that can carry a fact before concluding one is missing. Check company as well as associatedcompanyid.

Derive the domain's email convention from its sibling contacts. Twelve of the thirteen contacts at provenit.com use first-initial-plus-lastname; the one exception was the bounced record. An address that breaks its own domain's pattern is suspect before any vendor is consulted, and the pattern usually supplies the correction.

Metered · Sales Navigator

Confirm the seat on LinkedIn

Only for contacts stages 1–2 left unresolved, and only through the authorized account. Answers still-in-seat, tenure, and recent moves; writes ai__li_still_at_company, ai__li_recent_role_change, ai__li_tenure_years.

On the pilot list, 51 of the 60 unresolved contacts carry a LinkedIn URL — so one metered pass settles 85% of the questionable third.

Metered · vendor credits

Fill the remaining gaps — websites before vendors

Only the handful nothing above could answer, and in the order the census actually proved out: the company's own website first, then BBB's business-management block, then state and county contract PDFs, then Secretary of State filings, and only then ZoomInfo.

That ordering is not a preference, it is a measurement. The census sourced 294 new contacts across 124 dealers with company websites as the most productive source, while ZoomInfo failed to return an email for 121 of 150 decision-makers — an 81% miss on the one field that mattered most. Apply the rejection rules regardless: a company-only match scoring zero is empty, and a domain mismatch overrides any confidence number.

Websites lie too, just differently. One dealer's team page carried invented staff — "Robert Smith", "Christine Eve", "Mike Hardson" — theme placeholder text left live. Corroborate a name found on a website before trusting it.

Write · read-back verified

Stamp and route

Auto-write corrected emails, phones, titles, and the ai__ stamps — each read back and compared, because batch writes have reported success while silently failing. Queue for human sign-off: anything marking a person departed, and any delete or merge.

Set ai__contact_verified_date on everything touched. That date is what makes stage 6 cheap.

Free · the payoff

Emit channels, then re-run

Output a routing decision per contact, not a score. On the next run, stage 2 skips anything stamped inside the freshness window, so only new members and aged-out records cost anything. The first pass is the expensive one; every pass after is maintenance.

The output is a channel, not a score

A single quality grade forces bad decisions. A hard-bounced email says nothing about a phone; a flagged mobile says nothing about a direct line. Route each contact to the channel that is actually open.

Rules earned by getting it wrong

Each of these came from a real mistake in the pilot. They are the guardrails, and they are the reason the process is worth writing down.

Claimed a phone was "fine" having never checked one

Presence of a phone string was reported as reachability. Nothing in the run validated a single number.

State the source for every claim, or state that there is none. "Present" and "verified" are different words.
Declared three contacts to have no company

The check read associatedcompanyid and ignored the populated company field. All three were wrong; the true count was zero.

Before concluding a fact is absent, read every field that could hold it.
Read an email bounce as a departed person

Burfeind's address bounced because the CRM had invented the wrong pattern, not because he left.

A channel failure is evidence about the channel. Only tiers 1–3 speak to employment.
Consulted the inbox last instead of first

Outlook held the corrected email, the missing direct dial, and two meeting recaps — after vendor data had already been used to condemn the record.

First-party evidence runs first, every time. It is free, and it outranks everything purchased.
Presented a sample record as clean without checking it

The illustrative "clean" example turned out to have a flagged mobile as its only number.

Never hold up a record as representative until it has been through the same checks as the rest.
Trusted a stored LinkedIn URL as if it were proof of identity

Two of the first thirteen profiles checked were different people with the same name — a pharma VP standing in for a copier dealer's sales VP, and a Mars Petcare maintenance supervisor standing in for a ClearScale executive.

A profile is evidence only after its identity is corroborated on at least two of title, employer and city. Until then the URL is a guess, not a source.
Truncated a field, then reasoned from the fragment

Macri's job title named his real employer. It was clipped to 25 characters for display, and the answer was invisible for the rest of the investigation.

Never judge from a truncated value. Display short, decide on the full field.
Derived a field from another derived field

Personas are assigned from job titles by workflow. But the title field itself is written by enrichment automation that contradicts itself — one contact's title was rewritten four times in four months, flipping between two different jobs twice. Four separate workflows then compete to write the persona, two of them re-enrolling, and on several records two of them wrote different personas on the same day.

Never derive from a field you have not verified. Correcting the output of an unstable pipeline is wasted work — the pipeline overwrites it. Fix field ownership first, then the rules, then the records.
Broke on an apostrophe

A name-derivation routine crashed on "O'Dell" — the apostrophe terminated a string literal. In a bulk run it would have silently skipped every O'Brien, O'Neill and D'Angelo on the list.

Names contain apostrophes, hyphens, accents and single letters. Any routine that parses them needs a hostile test set before it touches a whole list.
Published a heuristic before measuring it

"Bounce plus a correctly-patterned address means the person left" was offered as a finding. Of ten checkable cases it was right three times. Six were still employed; one was the wrong person.

A heuristic prioritises what to look at. It never fills in the verdict column.

Diagnose the disease before choosing the treatment

Two lists were run through this pipeline and they failed in completely different ways. Reaching for the same tools on both would have wasted money on one and missed the problem on the other.

Symptom patternDiseaseWhat actually helps
Addresses that break their own domain's convention · records built by integrations · bounces on invented patterns Dirty Domain-convention repair, then one verification call per derived address. Cheap and high-yield.
Old list, filters never revisited · large never-contacted share · high-churn personas · bounces on correctly formed addresses Stale Employment verification only. Email tooling cannot touch this — the addresses are fine and the people have moved.
Titles that don't match the list's stated persona · members no filter should admit Mis-scoped Fix the list definition or the field it filters on. Needs no lookups at all — the cheapest of the three.

The tell that separates dirty from stale is subtle and worth naming: a bounce on a well-formed, convention-matching address means the person is gone; a bounce on a malformed one means the record is wrong. Same symptom, opposite cause, opposite treatment.

Where email verification actually belongs

An email verifier answers one question well — does this mailbox exist? — and cannot answer the one that matters most: will mail reach this human? Tested against three addresses that had genuinely hard-bounced, it returned "valid" for two of them; their mailboxes exist and still rejected real mail.

So never spend credits re-checking an address you already hold bounce data for. The bounce is ground truth and no verifier overrides it. Spend them validating addresses you just derived. On the one case that mattered it was decisive: it called the CRM's invented address invalid and the pattern-derived replacement valid, turning a guess into a verified address for a single credit. That is the whole job, and it is worth doing.

Carried over from the dealer census

The census ran this pipeline against 3,011 dealer contacts before it had a name. Four of its findings are load-bearing here.

Research that isn't stamped does not exist

262 dealer companies carry written verification notes and no ai__dealer_verdict. The work was done, the conclusion was reached, and every filter in the portal still reports them as unknown. That single number is the argument for stage 5: the verdict field, not the note, is the deliverable. A note is where you show your reasoning; the enum is what the next run reads.

Validation tools measure syntax, not employment

NeverBounce returned results for 2,912 of 3,011 contacts: 2,074 valid, 450 unknown, 386 catch-all — and 2 invalid. Two, out of nearly three thousand. That is not a clean list, it is a tool answering a different question than the one asked. It can tell you an address is well-formed and the domain accepts mail; it cannot tell you the person reading it left in March.

Some records are not yours to touch

53–56 companies are client accounts under hard hold and must not be modified without explicit sign-off, and 15 non-US dealer records are out of scope entirely. Any sweep that runs across a whole portal needs this exclusion list loaded before the first write, not discovered afterwards.

Name resolvers fail loudly in one direction and quietly in the other

The census recorded its name-based resolver producing large false-negative sets twice — reporting people missing who were present. This run added the mirror-image failure: name matches returning the wrong human, twice in thirteen. A resolver that can do both needs corroboration on every hit and a second pass on every miss.

CRM write mechanics that will waste an hour otherwise

  • Associations need two type IDs. PUT /crm/v4/objects/contacts/{id}/associations/default/companies/{cid} sets only typeId 279 on a fresh link. Without typeId 1 the calculated associatedcompanyid stays empty and the contact reads as orphaned. Send both explicitly: [{associationTypeId:1},{associationTypeId:279}].
  • Calculated fields lag. associatedcompanyid can take 20+ seconds to reflect a new association. Re-read before concluding a write failed.
  • Batch read does not accept propertiesWithHistory at 100 records. Drop to 25 per call, or the whole response returns null.
  • Property history is the audit trail. ?propertiesWithHistory=jobtitle reveals every writer and timestamp — the only way to catch two systems fighting over one field.
  • Verify every write by reading it back. Batch update returns COMPLETE with zero errors and can still not apply.

Withholding the verification stamp is the mechanism that makes the next run cheap and honest: refreshes skip what is stamped, so anything unresolved comes back around instead of quietly passing as clean.

Don't create these company records

Movers frequently land at solo advisory firms, fractional practices and one-person consultancies. Creating a company record for each adds exactly the junk the exercise exists to remove, and none of them are prospects. Record the destination in the evidence field and suppress the contact. Create a company only when the destination is a real target — and get its domain from the LinkedIn company page (website field) so the record is matchable later, never a name-only stub.

Disambiguating a destination before you move anyone

An email-domain change looks like a job change and usually isn't. Of 49 domain mismatches on one list, 37 were aliases — thomsonreuters.com/tr.com, pb.com/pitneybowes.com, former names like soxhub.com for AuditBoard, and five staff of one team on their league's shared domain. String similarity catches renames that look alike and misses the ones that don't; short domains and former names need a lookup. And never pick between same-named companies — three "Rally" records, six "PrismPath" records: suppress rather than guess.

Finding the address at the new employer

Added after the 5243 pass, when 42 of 70 re-associated movers turned out to have either no email at all or a dead one at the employer they had left. ZoomInfo could not supply a single one of them — it returned COMPANY_ONLY_MATCH, or a full match whose email field was simply absent. A vendor miss is where this ladder starts, not where it ends.

Rung 1 — look in the record you already have

Before deriving anything, sweep the other address fields on the contact: hs_additional_emails, email_2, email_other, work_email, linkedin__email. Two of the four hardest cases were solved here for the cost of one batch read. Stacy Malyil's stacys@aidoc.com had been sitting in hs_additional_emails the entire time while the primary Email field held a dead healthwise.org address. The CRM already knew.

Rung 2 — learn the company's format from a colleague you already trust

Search your own portal for a known-good address on the exact domain:

POST /crm/v3/objects/contacts/search
  filter: email CONTAINS_TOKEN "*@thedomain.com"

Score those samples against a pattern table — first.last, flast, first, firstlast, f.last, firstl — and keep only the patterns that actually explain a real sample. This solved 22 of 42 domains from data already owned, with zero external calls. Where the samples disagree, the domain has no single convention and you must not pretend otherwise.

Rung 3 — construct, then make the mail server answer

NeverBounce /v4/single/check is the arbiter. The four results are not shades of the same thing:

  • valid — the mailbox exists. Write it.
  • catchall — the domain accepts everything; the person is unconfirmed. Write it, flag it, never call it proven.
  • unknown — the server refuses verification altogether (Workday, Zendesk, Microsoft, UKG, dell.org). This proves nothing in either direction. Write only when the pattern is unambiguous from two or more samples, and stamp it UNVERIFIED.
  • invalid — a real negative, but only when that domain answers other probes. Then it is evidence, not a dead end.

Run these concurrently and in the background. A serial loop over forty contacts will exceed a two-minute shell timeout and teach you nothing except patience.

Rung 4 — read the negatives

An invalid from a domain that answers is a finding. Two cases, opposite conclusions:

  • Thomas Been. Every pattern invalid at domino.ai. The company had rebranded from Domino Data Lab and kept the old mail domain — thomas.been@dominodatalab.com is valid.
  • Kris Melville. Every pattern invalid at celigo.com, which answers. Five rejections and one acceptance later: she goes by Kris, not Kristin. When the server discriminates, keep trying nickname variants — the negatives are the map.

The hard rule, and the only two ways around it

An address is written only if its domain is the employer LinkedIn confirmed. A vendor-supplied address on the previous employer's domain is exactly the failure this whole process exists to prevent. Two exceptions, and they are not equally strong:

  • Same company, different domain. Accept when ZoomInfo resolves the alias to the identical company id — dominodatalab.com and domino.ai are both id 358094550.
  • Parent and division. Different ids, so weaker. hexagon.com is id 17662709; hexagonmi.com is id 396535383. A group that size runs one corporate mail domain, so accept — but only with a valid result and a note recording the weaker tier.

Where the old address goes

Never leave a former employer's address in the Email field and never simply delete it. Move it to previous__email, and its domain to previous__company_domain_name — which is a URL-type property and rejects a bare domain with INVALID_URL, so prefix https://. Read the existing value first and leave it alone if already set. And clearing a primary address takes two writes in order: empty hs_additional_emails, then empty email; otherwise HubSpot refuses with "Remove all secondary emails first."

Result on the 70 movers: 56 hold a confirmed current-employer address, 14 hold none with the old one filed, and none carry a prior employer's address. Before this pass, 18 did.

Two ways a good record still fails to reach a rep

Verification is not delivery. Both of these were discovered after the list was reported complete, which is the argument for reading membership back out of the list rather than trusting a local queue.

A filter token is a control character, not prose

The calling list excludes anyone whose evidence field contains the persona-exclusion token. When seven people were correctly reinstated as decision makers, the note explaining the reinstatement quoted that token verbatim — so the filter went on matching, and seven qualified prospects stayed invisible. Write the token only when you mean it, build it at runtime in scripts, and phrase every removal note without it.

Re-association can walk a contact off the source list

The calling list gates on membership in the source list, and the source list requires a known phone, a marketing persona, and membership in both HubSpot Tech Used and ZoomInfo — Contacts — the latter being currently_use_zoominfo_ = Yes. Move a contact to a new employer and they leave the ICP, so 46 of 70 movers dropped off. That is mostly the filter working: a marketing leader at a company using neither product is a different campaign.

The distinction that matters is between disqualified and unexamined. Of those 46, eighteen sit on enriched companies genuinely lacking the signal. Twenty-seven sit on company records created during the pass, whose ICP flags were never enriched at all — unproven, not negative. Creating a company record silently drops its occupants out of every tech-signal list until someone enriches it. Enrich on create, or accept that you are hiding people you just finished verifying.

Running it on the next list

The process takes a list ID and nothing else. It is deliberately indifferent to what the list is for — the reachability bar is a parameter, not a constant, because a cold prospecting list and a deal-follow-up list fail in different ways.

  • Scope. 1,634 lists in the portal, 189 carrying the CAS - prefix. Sizes range from 880 down to zero.
  • Start small. Prove a pass on a few hundred, inspect it, then scale. The pilot's corrections came from one contact being challenged.
  • Empty lists are their own defect. Five follow-up lists sit at zero members. That is broken filter logic, not bad data, and reps opening them see nothing.
  • Duplicates first. Lists 3625 and 4637 both hold exactly 183; 3274 and 4087 both hold 190. Validating the same population twice is pure waste.
